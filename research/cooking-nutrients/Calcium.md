# Calcium

Back to [[00 Index - Cooking and Nutrients]] · see also [[Iron]], [[Antinutrients]]

## Short answer

Calcium is a mineral; heat does not destroy it. Cooking changes it by:

1. **Leaching** into water that is discarded (calcium leaches less than potassium or sodium; usually a 0–25% loss).
2. **Releasing calcium from oxalate.** Boiling removes a big share of soluble oxalate from greens (30–87%, Chai & Liebman 2005), so more of the remaining calcium can be absorbed. See [[Antinutrients]].
3. **Softening bones.** Long cooking in vinegar (paksiw na isda), pressure cooking, and canning soften small fish bones so they are eaten. That **adds** a lot of calcium on the plate, compared with fish whose bones are left behind.

## Numbers
- Mineral retention after mass cooking: ~60–75% on average, biggest losses in boiled-and-squeezed vegetables (Kimura & Itokawa 1990).
- Boiling or steaming: 25–50% mineral loss; frying: almost none (Bognár 1998).

## Filipino angle
- **Dilis, tuyo, sardinas (canned), paksiw na galunggong** with bones eaten: very high calcium. The engine's `epPct` decides how much of the fish is eaten; for bone-in small fish cooked soft, a higher edible portion is justified. *This is an edible-portion question, not a retention question.*
- **Malunggay, saluyot, alugbati, kangkong** are calcium sources; **spinach-like oxalate-rich greens** (alugbati, kulitis/amaranth) give less absorbable calcium; boiling helps.
- **Bulalo / nilaga bone broth** adds little calcium unless vinegar is used and the broth is simmered for many hours. *Weak evidence; bone broths are usually low in calcium.*

## For SarapSulit
Retention factor ~**0.85–1.0**; ~**0.75–0.9** for vegetables boiled and drained; **1.0** with broth eaten.
