UP Oval Locker Locks

Each door gets a 12 V electronic cabinet lock, the same part used in parcel lockers. It stays locked with no power, springs open on a pulse shorter than a second, locks again when the door is pushed shut, and reports whether the door is closed. The lock itself is not smart. The kiosk computer decides when to open it.

Fail-secure means the lock stays locked without power. That is what you want for belongings: a blackout never opens a door, and the UPS or the terminal strip below still lets you open doors on purpose.

Wire colors differ between models; follow the supplier's diagram. The boards open one door at a time, so the 12 V supply only has to cover one pulse of up to 2 A on top of the Pi, screen and router.

What to buy

Ask the supplier for a lock that has

  • 12 V DC and fail-secure operation: locked with no power
  • Opening on a pulse, and relocking by itself when the door is pushed shut
  • A built-in feedback switch that reports the door or latch state
  • 2 A or less, rated for pulses (some allow up to 5 seconds on)
  • About 150 kg holding force, in zinc alloy or stainless steel
  • A mechanical override, key or pull cable, for emergencies
  • A stated cycle rating. Sellers quote up to 1,000,000; each door here opens about 450 times a year
  • A working temperature of 50 °C or more, since a steel box in the sun gets hot

And a control board that has

  • The same supplier as the locks, so voltage, current and feedback match
  • 24 or 48 channels, RS485, and a 12 V input
  • A published command protocol, so we can write our own driver
  • Door-status reading for every channel
  • Address switches, so two boards can share one cable

Order 4 locks and 1 board first. Buy the other 28 locks after the bench test passes.

Emergencies and security

Getting a door open when something fails

  • Power cut: nothing changes. The locks draw power only while opening, so the UPS keeps them working for hours.
  • Pi or board dead: at the terminal strip, touch a 12 V battery to that door's coil terminals for under a second.
  • Lock jammed: use its key or pull-cable override from inside the service cabinet.

Keeping it hard to break into

  • Lock and wires sit inside the steel frame; nothing is reachable from the front.
  • A folded lip on each door covers the gap where a screwdriver would go.
  • The switch reports every opening. A door that opens without a command sets off an alarm.
  • Five wrong PINs lock that door for 15 minutes (see the architecture page).

Bench test before buying 30

If you want to start with parts you already know, an ESP32 can drive the first 4 locks. Use a logic-level MOSFET or relay per lock and a diode across each coil. Common driver chips such as the ULN2803 cannot handle 2 A. For 30 doors, switch to the RS485 boards.

  1. Cycle test: open and relock each lock 1,000 times with a script. The switch must match every time.
  2. Heat test: run the cycle test with the unit in the afternoon sun.
  3. Power test: pull the plug during a rental. The doors must stay locked, run on the UPS, and open by battery at the terminal strip.
  4. Pry test: try a flat screwdriver at every door gap.
  5. Coil test: hold one lock open for 5 seconds and check that it does not overheat. The software should never hold it that long.
